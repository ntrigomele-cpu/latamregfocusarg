Offshore vs regulated platforms in Latin America

Argentina CNV PSAV registry as ground truth · Five deep cases · Status as of May 2026

Scope of this analysis

Geography: LatAm broadly. Argentina (CNV PSAV registry) and Brazil (BCB framework, effective Feb 2026) are the regulatory anchors. MX, CO, CL, PE referenced where relevant. · Included: International and cross-border platforms — crypto exchanges, FX/CFD brokers, payment platforms — whose strategic question is whether to formalize locally. · Excluded: Fully local compliance-first players (Lemon, Ripio, Belo, SatoshiTango) — they already answered the registration question. · Regulatory note: CNV PSAV covers crypto VASPs only. FX/CFD brokers (Exness, XM, FBS) fall under a different regulatory framework and are included for strategic comparison only.

Central insight

Argentina's PSAV registry already split the market in two

Argentina's CNV PSAV registry is the primary ground truth for this analysis — the most developed crypto registration framework in LatAm and the clearest dividing line between registered and offshore players. Marketing budgets, sponsorships and country managers don't change which side a player sits on. The list does.

Four ways to enter Argentina — what the nine cases reveal

Archetype 1 · Direct registration of the global entity

The global parent (or its named LatAm subsidiary) registers directly with the CNV. Used by Binance (Binance Services Latinoamérica), OKX (Sucursal Argentina), Coinbase (Bermuda Services), Kraken (Payward Trading), Bitget (BTGT Innovación), and WhiteBIT. Cleanest paper trail, highest reputational return. Standard play for established global brands with clean compliance posture.

Archetype 2 · Registration through a dedicated local SPV

Bybit's Luzpay S.A. is the textbook example. Create a clean Argentine corporate entity that operates locally, holds the PSAV registration, and ring-fences the parent from new disclosure obligations and audit triggers. Allows a player with a heavier global litigation profile to participate in the regulated club without exposing the global brand to additional jurisdictional risk. The most replicable play for players who can't or won't register their global entity directly.

Archetype 3 · Acquisition of a locally registered platform

Nexo's Buenbit acquisition is the only confirmed case, but the playbook is now public. Skip the registration timeline entirely by buying an already-compliant local entity. Bonus: gain users, local team, brand, and CUIT-verified KYC base in one move. Most expensive route, but also the fastest. Requires a willing seller — Buenbit had reached scale (1M+ users) but needed a global product layer they couldn't build themselves.

Archetype 4 · No registration, ride the offshore wave

Used by MEXC, BingX, KuCoin, HTX, Gate.io. Operate cross-border from offshore jurisdictions, localize the UX, invest heavily in marketing and KOLs, and bet that enforcement will remain selective. BingX is the extreme case: deepest Argentine community roots, most visited LABITCONF stand, most ambitious sports portfolio (Chelsea + Ferrari + Enzo Fernández) — all without a PSAV. Kraken's 2022–24 history is the cautionary tale for what this approach quietly costs when enforcement eventually arrives.

Six recurring findings across the nine cases

1 · Local rails matter more than local marketing. The single biggest functional gap between regulated and unregistered players isn't sponsorship spend — it's whether ARS deposits actually work via local payment partners. Bitget, Bybit (via Luzpay), Binance and Kraken all built local rails before scaling marketing. MEXC sponsors LABITCONF but Argentine users still rely on P2P workarounds. Marketing without rails is friction theater.
2 · The sequence "compliance → product → marketing" works. The reverse doesn't. Bitget started in 2022 (Messi), added compliance in 2024 (PSAV), scaled marketing in 2025 (LALIGA, Forlán). Bybit registered in August 2024 before appointing any CM. Binance registered in September 2024, then relaunched the Mastercard card in February 2025. MEXC inverted this — heavy marketing first, no compliance — and now faces structural exposure. The correct sequence is boring and slow; the wrong sequence looks fast and ages badly.
3 · Differentiated product wedges separate winners from incumbents. Binance's Pay+PIX integration. Kraken's Krak app and tokenized equities. Nexo's lending and yield products. Each picks a category that local champions (Lemon, Ripio, Belo, SatoshiTango) don't seriously compete in. Bitget and Bybit, by contrast, compete head-on with local players on spot trading and futures — which is harder, more expensive, and limits ceiling. The lesson: identify what locals can't do, then own that lane.
4 · Brand rehabilitation works better in LatAm than in the US or EU. Nexo used Argentina as the flagship of its post-SEC-settlement comeback. Binance used Argentina as proof that Mastercard would re-engage after the 2023 card cancellations. Kraken used it as proof that the post-FTX-cost-cutting company could still execute on global expansion. LatAm regulators are demanding but not punitive — which makes Argentina an attractive first stop for rebuild narratives that wouldn't fly in stricter jurisdictions.
5 · "Available globally from HQ" is not a market strategy. Kraken proved this the hard way between 2022 and 2024. Coinbase reached the same conclusion and announced a temporary exit from Argentina in January 2026 — less than a year after launching peso trading. The "we serve every country from the same platform" thesis collapses at the first encounter with capital controls, local payment infrastructure, or local language support. Every player still operating this way will eventually be forced into Kraken's correction or Coinbase's retreat.
6 · The IPO calendar is the ultimate forcing function. Kraken filed an S-1 and immediately had to convert "available there in theory" into "registered there in practice" for LatAm. Bitget is widely expected to follow. Public-market scrutiny doesn't tolerate loose geographic claims — every market a player names becomes a market they have to prove. For private competitors (MEXC, KuCoin) the lack of public scrutiny is a temporary advantage that disappears the moment they need outside capital or M&A optionality.

What predicts success in Argentina (in order of weight)

Across the nine cases analyzed, success correlates most strongly with:

1. A functioning local payment rail partner (single biggest predictor)

2. A CNV PSAV registration or equivalent regulatory standing

3. A differentiated product wedge that local players don't seriously compete in

4. A dedicated regional or country lead with local market context

5. Marketing and sponsorship spend

Note that marketing comes last. Every player who inverted this order — marketing first, the rest later — is currently either rebuilding (Kraken), exiting (Coinbase), or structurally exposed (MEXC). Every player who respected the order is in the regulated club.

Exness · Why include a non-crypto player

FX/CFD broker — different regulatory question, same offshore playbook

Exness is not a crypto exchange and does not appear in Argentina's CNV PSAV registry — which only governs Virtual Asset Service Providers. It is a multi-asset broker (Forex, CFDs including crypto CFDs, but not spot crypto), operating under FCA, CySEC, FSA Seychelles, Curaçao and BVI licenses. It is structurally offshore in every LatAm country.

Why this player matters for the LatAm offshore analysis

The argument for inclusion: Exness is the canonical example of how an unregulated international player operates a high-volume retail business in Latin America without ever becoming local. Its playbook (sport sponsorships, finfluencers, CPA affiliate engines, localized rails) is the template that crypto exchanges like MEXC are now copying — but in a regulatory environment far less forgiving than forex. Reading Exness clarifies what crypto offshore is borrowing from FX, and where it diverges.

The argument for caution: Exness is not registered as an ALyC (Liquidation and Compensation Agent) with CNV. It accepts Argentine users under its global licenses, operating directly through the internet under the laws of jurisdictions where it is registered (Curaçao, BVI). Clients are protected by Exness's own compensation funds — not local investor protection regimes. The same logic applies in Brazil, Mexico, Chile and Colombia: clients are governed by offshore jurisdictions, not local law.

Time horizon: Forex CFD regulation in LatAm remains a grey zone in most countries. No equivalent to Brazil's BCB PSAV exists for retail FX brokers. That gives Exness more time than crypto exchanges — but the playbook is the same, and watching what regulators do about FX next will preview what happens to crypto.
